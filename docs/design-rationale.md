# FocusForge — Design Rationale

A catalog of the hand-tuned decisions that shape how FocusForge looks
and feels. Not a comprehensive design system spec — a record of the
choices worth defending, with the values and reasoning attached.

The point isn't that any individual choice here is novel. It's that
each one was *chosen* — not inherited from a starter template, not
generated by a model, not pulled from a UI kit. The compound of small
deliberate choices is what gives the app its texture.

---

## 1. Two emotional registers

The app has two modes with deliberately opposite atmospheres:

**Focus mode.** Near-black canvas (`#0A0A0F`). One accent color — the
session's progress ring. The character is absent. No animation beyond
the ring's slow fill. The screen reads as a tool, not entertainment.
This is what the user looks at for 25 minutes; it has to be quiet enough
to disappear.

**Reward mode.** Deep purple gradient (`#0C0820 → #241850 → #0C0820`).
Layered radial glows. 16 particles drifting. The character is present,
lit dramatically. The reward beats land here. The screen reads as
celebration, not utility.

The contrast between restraint and richness IS the dopamine hit. A
single-mode app — atmospheric throughout, or stark throughout — flattens
the emotional arc of a focus session. By making the canvas itself
transform when the work completes, the work is what causes the
transformation.

**Implementation:** `FFTheme.Background.primary` vs.
`FFTheme.Background.rewardTop/Mid/Bottom`. The crossfade happens in
beat 2 of `RewardOverlayView` over 600ms.

## 2. The timer ring is three composed layers

A default `Circle().trim(from:to:).stroke()` is a single line. The
FocusForge timer ring is three:

- **Track** at `Color.white.opacity(0.04)` — barely visible, but
  important: without it, the ring looks like it's floating against
  nothing. The track grounds the geometry.
- **Aura** — the same color as the ring, at `0.12` opacity when idle,
  `0.24` opacity when running, `0.10` opacity when paused. Drawn with
  a wider stroke (~10pt vs the crisp ring's 3pt). The aura is what
  makes the ring read as "glowing."
- **Crisp** — the same color, at `0.70` / `0.95` / `0.50` opacity for
  idle / running / paused. Thin (3pt). This is the line that conveys
  exact progress.

When the timer is paused, both layers soften (aura 0.10, crisp 0.50) so
the screen reads as "frozen mid-session" without changing layout. When
running, the aura is pushed wider by 2pt for a subtle "breathing"
presence.

This wasn't a default that came with SwiftUI. It was three rings,
manually composed, with state-dependent opacities tuned by eye until
the screen felt right. ~80 lines in `GlowProgressRingView.swift`.

## 3. The five-beat reward sequence

When a session completes, the screen doesn't slide up a notification
card. It transforms. The transformation runs in five beats, 400ms apart:

| Beat | t | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0ms | Ring pulses on the timer position (`RingPulseView`, 600ms ease-out) |
| 2 | 400ms | Background crossfades from near-black to deep purple (600ms ease-out) |
| 3 | 800ms | Particles drift in. Checkmark scales in. Headline + minute count + streak badge slide up from the bottom edge (spring, bounce 0.15) |
| 4 | 1200ms | Reward card slides up. XP and coin numbers count up from 0 over 800ms |
| 5 | 1600ms | "Continue" CTA fades in from the bottom (300ms ease-out) |

**Total runtime: ~1.9 seconds.** Tap anywhere to skip to final state. The
sequence respects `accessibilityReduceMotion` — when on, all five beats
collapse into a single 300ms cross-fade with no motion.

Why five beats instead of one transition? The eye can only register one
moment of change at a time. Crossfading the background while
simultaneously animating in the checkmark and counting up the XP would
feel like a single overloaded moment — visually noisy, but emotionally
flat. Staging the beats means each one lands.

**Implementation:** `RewardOverlayView.runFullSequence()` is an async
function that `Task.sleep`s between beats. Each beat uses an explicit
`withAnimation` block tuned to the right easing curve.

## 4. WCAG AA contrast, measured not guessed

Most dark-mode apps pick text colors by eye. FocusForge measured every
text color against the actual background:

| Token | Color | Contrast on `Background.primary` |
|---|---|---|
| `Text.primary` | white at 0.92 opacity | **16.57:1** (AAA) |
| `Text.secondary` | white at 0.50 opacity | **5.30:1** (AA) |
| `Text.tertiary` | white at 0.50 opacity | **5.30:1** (AA) |
| `Text.disabled` | white at 0.15 opacity | 1.46:1 (exempt — disabled state, WCAG 1.4.3) |

The original style guide used white at 0.30 opacity for tertiary text
on a near-black background — that fails AA (2.46:1). Catching this
required pulling out a contrast calculator and testing pairs. Hierarchy
between secondary and tertiary is now preserved through typography
(size and weight) rather than opacity, since dropping below ~0.45 fails
AA Normal on near-black surfaces.

Same exercise for the **rarity colors** in the cosmetic system:

- Common (`#888888`) — 5.57:1, passes
- Rare — originally `#9B59B6` at 4.23:1, **failed AA**. Brightened to
  `#B07DCB` for 6.25:1.
- Animated Rare (`#E67E22`) — 6.93:1, passes

These ratios live as a comment block in `FFTheme.swift` so any future
edit to a text color has the contrast bar visible.

## 5. The coach catalog is a manuscript

The "AI Coach" is 33 hand-written templates — 15 framing, 10 reflection,
8 nudge — each authored in three tones (encouraging, direct, calm).
That's about **99 distinct pieces of micro-copy**, all written by one
person.

This was a deliberate choice against the dominant pattern of wrapping a
frontier model. A frontier model would generate variety effortlessly,
but the variety would be uncontrolled. Anyone who has seen "Great job!
Keep up the awesome work! 🌟" from an AI coach has seen what happens
when a model with no taste owns the output: every encouragement reads
like every other, polished to a sheen with no edges.

A handwritten catalog trades infinite generation for finite,
controllable variety. The encouraging variant exclaims. The direct
variant commands. The calm variant invites. Each variant is in a
distinct voice because a human wrote it in that voice. The user can
read the entire decision surface of the coach in a single open-source
file: [`CoachTemplateCatalog.swift`](https://github.com/kristenmartino/focusforge-coach-engine/blob/main/Sources/FocusForgeCoachEngine/Catalog/CoachTemplateCatalog.swift).

**Below the catalog**, a 10-line safety filter rejects shaming patterns
("you failed," "lazy," "pathetic," etc.) and caps output at 300
characters. It's a defense-in-depth artifact — the bundled catalog
doesn't trip it — but it's there so downstream consumers extending the
catalog can't accidentally introduce hostile copy.

## 6. Streak design without shame

Most habit apps weaponize streaks. "You lost your streak!" becomes
the dominant emotion the user associates with the app — guilt, loss,
sunk-cost panic. The metric optimizes itself and decouples from the
underlying goal.

FocusForge's streak design refuses that pattern at three points:

- **Streak freezes earned at milestones** (day 3, 7, 14, 30, 60). Miss
  a day and a freeze is automatically consumed to protect the streak.
  The user doesn't have to remember to use it. The freeze is invisible
  insurance, not a panic button.
- **No leaderboards. No comparisons. No friend-streak features.** The
  streak is a private relationship between the user and their
  character, not a competitive metric.
- **The streak-rescue nudge** (when the streak is at risk) doesn't say
  "Don't lose your streak!" Templates open with "Keep it going!" /
  "A gentle reminder" / "Your streak matters." The verb is
  invitation, not threat. Copy varies by tone preference, but no
  template in the catalog uses shame.

Streaks are still tracked, still surfaced, still celebrated at
milestones. The character grows with the streak. But the failure
mode — "I lost my streak so I quit" — is treated as a design output,
not a user behavior. The freeze + no-shame nudge + no-leaderboard
compound is the structural rejection of that failure mode.

## 7. The spacing scale is geometric, not arbitrary

FFTheme.Spacing is an 8-step scale: **4 / 8 / 12 / 16 / 20 / 24 / 32 /
40** (`xxs` through `xxxl`). Not Fibonacci, not a strict 1.5×
geometric series — a near-doubling early, then opening up wider at the
top. Tight at the micro-scale where pixels matter (padding inside a
pill, gap between an icon and a label), looser at the layout scale
(section margins, sheet horizontal padding).

Corner radius follows the same logic: **6 / 10 / 16 / 24** (`sm`
through `xl`). Small enough for pill rewards (6), big enough for
sheets (24), with two mid-points for cards and buttons.

Every padding and every corner in the app references one of these
tokens. There's no raw `padding(20)` anywhere — it's
`.padding(FFTheme.Spacing.lg)`. The constraint produces visual rhythm
across screens without anyone having to consciously enforce it.

## 8. The character has weight

The character sprite isn't a flat illustration on a flat background.
In the dressing room:

- A **ground plane** draws a thin light line under the character with
  a subtle underglow — implying floor without rendering one. Without
  the ground plane the character floats in negative space, which reads
  as "render bug," not "minimalist."
- A **CharacterSceneBackground** layers a dark gradient with an
  ambient character glow that picks up the equipped horns/wings.
- The character's **animated rare** cosmetics (Ram Curls, Ray Gun) use
  a hand-tuned shimmer animation that pulses over ~1.5 seconds. The
  shimmer is what differentiates "I own this item" from "I unlocked
  this item." Common and rare items don't shimmer.

The compound of these three small effects — ground plane, ambient
glow, shimmer for animated rares — is what gives the dressing room its
physical weight. Each effect is small. Together they make a stage.

## What's not in this doc

Decisions still being tuned (not yet locked in code):

- Final easing curves on the milestone unlock animation
- Final reward-card haptic intensity
- Final particle field behavior over 30s+ session completions

Things that exist in the app but aren't called out here because they're
table stakes, not craft signals: dark mode forced via
`.preferredColorScheme(.dark)`, dynamic type support on every text
view, VoiceOver labels on every interactive element. These should be
in *every* iOS app and aren't differentiators.

---

## Why write this down

Two reasons:

1. **As a forcing function.** Writing the rationale forces the
   decisions to be defensible. A choice that can't be written down in
   2-3 sentences probably isn't a choice — it's a default that snuck
   in.

2. **As an artifact.** The portfolio narrative for FocusForge isn't
   "I built a Pomodoro app." It's "I made a hand-built thing." Hand-built
   is a claim. This doc is one of several proofs.

The repo has other proofs: the
[open-source coach engine](https://github.com/kristenmartino/focusforge-coach-engine)
(99 lines of human-written micro-copy), the
[App Store listing](app-store-listing.md) (drafted with the same craft
voice as the app), the
[test suite](../app/FocusForgeTests/) (46 tests across critical paths).

A craft claim is only credible when there are artifacts. The artifacts
are what.
